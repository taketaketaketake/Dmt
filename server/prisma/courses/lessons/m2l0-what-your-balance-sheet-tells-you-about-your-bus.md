## What does Your Company’s Balance Sheet Tell You About Your Business

> **In this Topic, you will learn:**

- Assets = What your company owns
- How you should leverage the company’s Assets
- What types of Liabilities are outstanding and how many are current (due within 1 year)
- Do you have sufficient working capital or do you need to seek financing or contribute more capital
- Is your Equity in the company growing or decreasing
- Are you reinvesting or withdrawing profit

## Assets = What your company owns

Assets are organized in two broad categories, each of which can be further organized into various sub-categories:

:::compare
### Current Assets
Cash and assets that can be easily converted into cash, such as: Cash Equivalents, Accounts Receivable, Inventory, and the current portion of Notes Receivable.
### Long-Term Assets
Assets that are not easily converted into cash, such as: Machines, Fixed Assets, Intangible Assets (licenses, intellectual property), and Buildings.
:::

- An asset may be **depreciated** (expensed) over time, so that its recorded cost gradually declines over its useful life. An example is a building, which may be depreciated over many years
- Alternatively, an asset may be recorded at its **full value** until such time as it is consumed. An example is a prepaid expense, which will be converted to expense as soon as it is consumed
- The one type of asset that is not considered to be consumed and is not depreciated is **land**. The land asset is presumed to continue in perpetuity

An asset is an expenditure that has utility through multiple future accounting periods. For example, your company buys a machine which you expect to use for the next five years — since this expenditure has utility through multiple future periods, it is recorded as an asset.

## How can you leverage the company’s Assets

There are two main ways that you can leverage your company’s assets:

:::compare
### Operational Leverage
Use your company’s assets to generate revenue, increase cash flows and returns.
### Financial Leverage
Use your company’s assets to take on debt or raise capital — to increase assets, manage cash flows, or reduce or retire more costly debt.
:::

Both methods are accompanied by risk, such as insolvency, but can be very beneficial to a business.

## What types of Liabilities are outstanding, and how much is current

- A liability is something a person or company owes, usually a sum of money
- Liabilities are incurred when you receive money, service, or product in exchange for a promise to pay at a later date

There are two broad categories of Liabilities:

:::compare
### Current Liabilities
Debt obligations that are due in 1 year or less — accounts payable, current portion of outstanding loans or notes payable, payroll liabilities, tax liabilities, credit card charges, line of credit drawdowns, and accrued expenses.
### Long-Term Liabilities
Debt obligations that are due more than 1 year away — for example, the portions of a loan or note that mature in more than 1 year.
:::

## Do you have sufficient working capital?

Working Capital is money available to pay for the operations of the company.

:::formula
Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities
Net Working Capital = Current Assets (less cash) − Current Liabilities (less debt)
Net Working Capital = Accounts Receivable + Inventory − Accounts Payable
:::

Understanding and proactively managing your Working Capital enables effective management of your company’s short-term liquidity — for example, you can anticipate when you’ll need to seek financing when your accounts payable are greater than your accounts receivable.

## Is your Equity in the company growing or decreasing

There are many factors that affect your Equity in your company:

:::cards
- Your Equity **increases** when the company is profitable, and you are retaining or reinvesting the profit into the business
- Your Equity **increases** when you add additional money into your business
- Your Equity **decreases** when the debt used to finance operations, capital expenditures, working capital, etc. is greater than the capital you invested in the company
- Your Equity **decreases** when the company is not profitable, and no new capital is being raised to make the company profitable
- Your Equity **decreases** when you withdraw money from the company
:::

## Are you reinvesting or withdrawing profit?

What can — and should — you do with your company’s profit?

The Equity section of the Balance Sheet lets you know how well you are leveraging your company’s remaining Assets after satisfying all the company’s outstanding obligations.

:::reveal What does the Equity section signal to investors?
- Increases in Retained Earnings or Accumulated Earnings plus no change in withdrawals or dividend distributions demonstrate that you are **reinvesting** your company’s profit to grow the business.
- Increases in withdrawals or dividend distributions can indicate to investors that you are **not** using your company’s profit to invest in your business.
:::

## Summary

> **In this Topic, you learned:**

- The Balance Sheet is organized to show what the company owns (Assets), what it owes to vendors and other debtors (Liabilities), and what’s left over for the owners, if any (Equity)
- There are two kinds of leverage: operating and financial
- Working Capital is used to proactively manage the company short-term liquidity to ensure that there is money to pay for the company’s operations
- Equity is affected by several factors: retained earning, withdrawals, dividend distributions, and capital contributions
- Investors look at the Equity activities to gauge whether you are reinvesting in your company or not
