## What are performance analytics and why do you need them

- Performance analytics are measures or ratios that explain or show the relationship between different parts of your company’s operations and how well they are meeting your goals
- For example, the Return on Assets ratio lets you know how well the company’s assets are being used to generate revenue and profit. If revenue and profit are greater than total assets, then your assets are meeting or exceeding your goals
- To truly understand the real sources of value in your company, you must have measures and analysis; otherwise, it’s all guess work

## What analytics can be calculated from the magic three reports

- Some of the biggest analytics can be calculated from the balance sheet, profit and loss statement, and cash flows statement.
- You can measure your company’s health by using the various type of business and financial ratios:
- Profitability Ratios – measures the company’s ability to generate profit, e.g., profit margin, operating margin
- Liquidity Ratios – measures the company’s ability to pay its current debts without raising external capital, e.g., acid test ratio, operating cash flow ratio, and current ratio
- Activity (Key) Ratios – illustrates the current financial condition of the company, e.g., working capital ratio, return on assets, return on equity, and turnover ratios
- Financial Risk Ratios – measures the company’s ability to service their debts, e.g., debt to capital ratio, debt to equity ratio, and degree of combined leverage ratio
- Solvency Ratios – measures the company’s ability to service long term debt, e.g., interest coverage ratio, debt to asset ratio, and equity ratio

## Other sources of information used to calculate performance ratios

- These other sources provide supplemental information that is rolled up into the Magic Three reports.
- Other sources of information include:
  - Accounts Receivable Aging Report – A/R Turnover ratio
  - Accounts Payable Aging Report – A/P Turnover ratio
  - Payroll Reports – Employee to Revenue Ratio; Payroll Turnover ratio
  - Inventory Reports – Inventory Turnover ratio
  - Marketing & Sales Reports – ROI of Marketing; Sales ratios

## Examples of using performance analytics to make better decisions about your company’s operations

- Operating Margin - The operating margin measures how much profit a company makes on a dollar of sales after paying for variable costs of production, such as wages and raw materials, but before paying interest or tax.*
  - Formula: Operating Income / Net Sales = Operating Margin
    - Higher ratios are generally better, illustrating the company is efficient in its operations and is good at turning sales into profits.*
- Operating Cash Flow Ratio - The operating cash flow ratio indicates if a company's normal operations are sufficient to cover its near-term obligations.*
  - Formula: Operating Cash Flow / Current Liabilities = Operating Cash Flow Ratio
    - A higher ratio means that a company has generated more cash in a period than what was immediately needed to pay off current liabilities.*
- Accounts Receivable Turnover - The receivables turnover ratio measures the efficiency with which a company collects on its receivables or the credit it extends to customers. The ratio also measures how many times a company's receivables are converted to cash in a period.*
  - Formula: Net Credit Sales / Average Accounts Receivable
    - A high receivables turnover ratio may indicate that a company’s collection of accounts receivable is efficient and that the company has a high proportion of quality customers that pay their debts quickly.*
    - A company’s receivables turnover ratio should be monitored and tracked to determine if a trend or pattern is developing over time. Also, companies can track and correlate the collection of receivables to earnings to measure the impact the company’s credit practices have on profitability.*

## Example of using performance analytics in securing financing

- Debt to Equity Ratio - It is a measure of the degree to which a company is financing its operations through debt versus wholly owned funds. More specifically, it reflects the ability of shareholder equity to cover all outstanding debts in the event of a business downturn.*
  - Formula: Total Liabilities / Total Shareholders’ Equity = Debt to Equity Ratio
    - Higher-leverage ratios tend to indicate a company or stock with higher risk to shareholders.*
    - Investors will often modify the D/E ratio to focus on long-term debt only because the risks associated with long-term liabilities are different than short-term debt and payables.*
- Total Debt to Total Asset Ratio - The total-debt-to-total-assets ratio shows the degree to which a company has used debt to finance its assets.*
  - Formula: Total Short-Term Debt + Long-Term Debt / Total Assets = Total Debt to Total Equity Ratio
    - Investors use the ratio to evaluate whether the company has enough funds to meet its current debt obligations and to assess whether the company can pay a return on its investment. *
    - Creditors use the ratio to see how much debt the company already has and whether the company can repay its existing debt. This will determine whether additional loans will be extended to the firm.*
    - A ratio greater than 1 shows that a considerable portion of the assets are funded by debt. In other words, the company has more liabilities than assets.*
    - A high ratio also indicates that a company may be putting itself at risk of defaulting on its loans if interest rates were to rise suddenly.*
